Epigraphy · glyph system · 16 × 16, continuous

The glyph
system

Every symbol below is generated live from its Latin lemma by the rule on this page.

Nothing here is hand-drawn. Each glyph is 16 × 16 pixels — Minecraft's own item resolution — carved into a stone tile and scaled up. What you see is exactly what ships.

A glyph is two stacked letter-forms standing on a foot that tallies the word's letters, chiselled into an octagonal stone tile and inlaid with a pigment hashed from the word's own name.

Each letter is its own figure — a gable, a thorn, a grate, a diamond, a coffer, a saltire — built from five carved parts: the stave, the bar, the diagonal, the chevron and the stone. What varies is which parts a letter uses and how they join.

Anatomy

Rules that had to go

The shared skeleton was the real problem. While every letter was one upright with a mark hung off it, roughly twelve of seventeen pixels were identical in every letter — only four carried identity, and 28 of 210 letter pairs differed by a single arm. The fix was to invert that ratio: each letter is now its own figure, not one figure with a different flourish.

Mirror symmetry had to go so a letter could lean, corner, or carry its weight on one side. Forced continuity went too — the horns and the three stones are drawn in separate pieces, and at this size detached marks read cleanly.

The frame is gone too. At 16 × 16 a border costs a quarter of the usable area and was never carrying identity. Removing it bought the room that made 16 × 16 viable at all.

The foot tallies the letters. Word length sets the foot's width and its serif — countable, and it separates words sharing their first two letters.

Carved stone, and the blank

Each tile is an octagon — the 16 × 16 square with its corners chamfered by two — so the silhouette reads as a cut stone rather than a sprite. Outside the octagon the texture is transparent.

Depth comes from one height field and one light. Void sits at −1, the incised stroke at 0, the stone surface at 1; every pixel is then compared against its three up-left and three down-right neighbours, diagonals double-weighted. Higher than up-left turns into the light; higher than down-right turns away. That single rule produces all of it — the bevelled rim of the tile, the chamfered corners, the shadowed wall on the upper-left of every groove and the lit wall on its lower-right. Nothing is special-cased, and a two-pixel stem comes out as a true V-groove: dark left wall, bright right wall.

Nothing comes within three pixels of the stone's edge, chamfered corners included. That is a measured constraint, not an eyeballed one: the audit takes every cut pixel, walks outward to the nearest transparent pixel, and fails the build if any is closer than three. It is the tightest constraint in the whole system — it sets the chamfer, sets the tally's ceiling, and is why the two letter-forms share a row.

The fourth tile is the blank glyph: bare stone, no cuts. It is what an unknown glyph looks like, what an empty codex slot holds, and what an uninscribed tablet shows.

The letter forms

Twenty-one letters, twenty-one figures. Not one skeleton with variations — that was the mistake behind every earlier table. Each letter is built from a small shared vocabulary of carved parts: the stave (an upright), the bar (a lintel), the diagonal, the chevron, and the stone (a 2 × 2 block).

What varies is which parts a letter uses and how they join — meeting at a point, crossing, cornering, closing, or floating free. Never size, and never a one-row offset: those are the differences a reader can't see without both letters in front of them.

Every pair is sayable out loud. The diamond against the coffer. Roof on the post against floor under the post. Two bars against three. Strokes against stones.

Twenty-one is the classical Latin alphabet. J, U and W are mediaeval; Y and Z were Greek imports Latin had long absorbed as I and S, so a stray Y folds to I and Z to S, exactly as PULVIS becomes PVLVIS.

Every earlier table drew outlines, and that was the mistake. Vessels, boxes, doubled rings — and each rebuild made them more elaborate to keep them apart. Wrong direction: an outline is a picture, and pictures must be intricate to differ. Writing is not made of outlines. It is made of strokes.

Carved scripts settled this long ago, under exactly these constraints — hard material, small size, must be unmistakable. Elder Futhark is an upright with one or two branches off it and nothing curved, because curves are miserable to cut. Ogham reduces it further still. Borrowing the structure is not a stylistic nod; it is the answer to the same engineering problem.

The numbers, before and after. The stave alphabet had 28 of 210 letter pairs differing by 4 pixels — one arm — and the whole alphabet spanned only 4–16 px, which is why it read as twenty-one variations on one letter. The figures span 12–34 px, with a median near 21 and nothing at all below 12. Mean ink went down, 17 → 15: this was not bought with density.

Watch the measuring instrument, not just the design. Every rebuild here was misled by one at some point — a pixel threshold calibrated for dense outlines that silently excluded every light form; a row-width test that couldn't see which side a mark was on; and a contact sheet still mirroring asymmetric letters after symmetry had been dropped, which made a whole review worthless. A metric that agrees with you is worth re-deriving.

Four things fall out for free, which is how you know the abstraction fits: the stave spans the full height and holds the centre top and bottom, so stacked forms link with nothing to check; a mirrored branch is simply a chevron, so the symmetry axis costs nothing; arms are clamped per row, so none can crowd the stone's edge; and a form is about 20 pixels against 24–30 for the outlines.

Two letters, not three. 16 × 16 has a quarter the pixels of 32 × 32, and three six-row zones will not fit. Two do — and uniqueness survives because the foot tallies word length: two words that share both marked letters still separate on length. In the shipping 52 no two runes share a mark at all, so the foot is headroom for growth rather than what holds the lexicon apart.

The foot is a tally. The bar widens one step per letter up to three; past three its ends turn up into a serif — the hand mark — and the width starts counting again, so letters = 2 + (width − 1) + 3 × serif. Six readable lengths out of a bar that never exceeds three half-widths, which is what holds the widest foot three pixels clear of the chamfer. Six is fewer than it sounds: the whole lexicon still renders 49 distinct tiles, because the forms do the heavy lifting.

The two forms overlap by one row. Both are already obliged to occupy the centre columns at their top and bottom row, so sharing that row is exactly where they were going to link anyway — it costs nothing and buys the row the margin needs.

The cut, and what fills it

Every groove is cut to the same depth and filled with the glyph's own pigment — a colour hashed straight out of the lemma. Blood Magic's runes are the reference here: a symbol sunk into stone, its inlay doing the work of telling one rune from another at a glance across a room.

Two sources, in order. Static — an authored pigment on the glyph, chosen by hand for the word it names. Dynamic — FNV-1a over the lemma, the low bits becoming the hue. Anything missing, malformed or out of range falls through to the hash silently: a bad hex must never be able to fail a datapack load.

The fallback isn't a degraded mode. It's what lets a modder add fifty glyphs in an afternoon and get stable, per-word colour with no art decisions at all — the same lemma is the same colour in every world, forever, with nothing stored. The static layer exists so the words that deserve a colour get the right one.

Either way the result is renormalised to one fixed luminance (62 of 255, against stone that runs 110–195). A yellow groove and a blue groove therefore cut exactly as deep. The author picks the hue; the renderer keeps the depth — which is what stops a hand-picked palette from quietly breaking the guarantee below. Measured across the lexicon the spread is 61.6 – 62.4, under one percent.

Colour is still carrying nothing (D16). Desaturate the whole atlas and all 49 glyphs stay distinct — verified, not asserted. The pigment is redundant reinforcement: a second, faster channel onto an identity that shape already carries in full. Two glyphs are even allowed to share a hue, and two tools nearly do; nothing breaks, because nothing was resting on it.

The lexicon — 26 axes, 52 runes

Every rune in the language, drawn by the rule above, with its opposite beside it and its English under it. ✦ marks a rune that may head a word — that names a kind of thing rather than qualifying one. Each carries a static pigment chosen for family, not brightness: luminance is normalised away, so only the hue survives.

The axis is itself a concept. PLENVM/VACVVM is fullness; NOVVM/SENEX is age. Learn one pole and you have half-learned the other, which makes 52 runes far cheaper to teach than 52 words would suggest — and it is why a new rune is admissible only if it completes an axis.

And because colour is redundant, a shared band costs nothing. Ten implements in one grey doesn't make two tools ambiguous; their shapes were already doing the work.

Why an octagon, and not n sides per letter

Tying the side count to word length is a tempting rule, and it is the wrong variable. Three reasons, in order of how much they hurt:

1 · Length is already spoken for. The foot tallies it. Spending the silhouette — the single most visible feature at inventory scale — on a variable that is already encoded buys nothing, while the variable that isn't encoded goes on wanting a channel.

2 · 16 px cannot hold the alphabet of shapes. A square, an octagon and a heavy-chamfer octagon are the three silhouettes that survive at this size, and you can see all three above. Pentagons, heptagons and nonagons are indistinguishable mush at 16 px, and any odd-sided polygon either breaks the vertical mirror axis or eats the rows the figure needs — a true hexagon points top and bottom, exactly where the letter-forms and the foot live.

3 · Latin lengths cluster. Nearly the whole lexicon sits at four to seven letters, so most glyphs would land on the same two or three shapes regardless. The encoding would read as random rather than systematic — the worst outcome for a system whose whole promise is that it is legible.

The variable actually worth the silhouette is grammatical class. The head rule turns on which glyphs can act as determinatives, and that is what a player parses first when guessing a rune word. Three or four silhouettes — thing, place, condition — would put the grammar on the outline where it can be read across a wall of carvings. That is a real proposal and it is not in v1: one universal octagon ships first, because a class encoding wants the lexicon's classes settled before it is frozen into art.

Six words, and the two axes behind them

The tinted lemma in each is the head — the last determinative-capable glyph, which fixes the word's category. Amber-ruled cards introduce a new glyph.

Zero new glyphs bought all six. The whole ore → dross → ingot chain is one refinement axis (CANDIDVM/SORDES) pointed at a metal, so it works for iron, gold, copper and anything added later with no vocabulary at all.

Opposites come free. Illager and Villager are the same word with one pole swapped. That is not a saving of one glyph — it is the reason a player who decodes either one has effectively decoded both.

Nine tests

Every one of these is built from the 52, and nothing was minted to make them work. That is the test of an axis language: if the axes are the right ones, the words are already there.

…and the longest word in the language

Heads travel. HOMO heads six creature words, VALLVM heads the fortress, the city and the mansion. Decoding one gives you most of the rest — which is what makes an axis lexicon cheap to learn rather than merely small.

Compounds are literal. A city is a throng's wall, so End City needs no word for "city". A hive is a made hollow for a swarm — and dropping OPVS turns the hive into the natural nest, which is exactly the distinction Minecraft draws.

Rune words are free-length. The stairs are one word of five glyphs, not a phrase. The head rule is defined by position — the last ✦ glyph — so it reads two glyphs and six identically. What limits length is a budget, not a grammar rule: the codex holds 20 glyphs for a whole inscription, so a long name crowds out the other clauses.

Blocks and materials

The head is tinted. Note ORDO · PVLVIS for redstone — powdered law. No lexicon built by listing nouns would have reached it; an axis lexicon has no other way to say it.

Obsidian's word is its recipe. IGNIS · VNDA · SAXVM — fire meeting water in stone. Nothing about that was designed; it is what the axes say when you point them at the block.

Prismarine and Dripstone show the failure mode too. Both wanted VNDA · SAXVM. One ordered sequence must name one thing, so the dripstone takes a qualifier — VNDA · GRADVS · SAXVM. A collision inside the language is a content bug, caught at load by the same validator that catches two glyphs rendering alike.

Made things — the tool family falls out

There is no rune for "tool", "pickaxe" or "blade". A tool names what it cuts, then the material it is: [object] · FRACTVM · [material]. The material is the head, so tier and type stay independent and every combination in the game is already spelled.

A player who decodes LIGNVM · FRACTVM · FERRVM (Iron Axe) and SAXVM · FRACTVM · ADAMAS (Diamond Pickaxe) can read SAXVM · FRACTVM · FERRVM on sight, having never seen it. The entire tool tree costs zero new vocabulary — it is the cutting axis pointed at things that already had names.

A vessel is a made void. The bucket and the chest need no word for "container": OPVS · VACVVM is one, and the qualifier says what kind. That is an axis doing a noun's job, which is the whole design.

Creatures — one head, many qualifiers

Most creatures are [quality] · BESTIA, and the upright kinds take HOMO instead. Every qualifier here was already in the lexicon for materials, places or rituals — nothing was minted for any creature.

Taming flips one pole and changes nothing else. A wild wolf is HOSTIS · BESTIA, a tamed one MITIS · BESTIA. The language says out loud what the game means by taming — and Illager/Villager is the same trick on the other head.

Silence is meaningful. The Enderman carries no temper pole — neither foe nor friend — because it is neither until you look at it. An unmarked axis is a statement, which is only possible in a lexicon where every rune has an opposite.

Knowledge is drawn, not tinted

Colour never carries anything on its own. Identity comes from the two letter-forms, length from the foot tally, and what the player knows from how much of the glyph is cut at all. Desaturate the atlas and nothing becomes ambiguous — the pigment is a second channel onto an identity that shape already carries in full.

Tier is depth, not tint. Unknown is uncut stone. Sighted is a shallow, unfilled scratch — the whole figure is there, but the groove is weak and holds bare stone-grey instead of the word's pigment: you have seen the carving and not yet taken the rubbing. Learned is cut to full depth and inlaid.

Because the ladder is uncut → shallow and empty → deep and filled, it is a value difference before it is a colour one. It survives desaturation, and it reads identically to a colourblind player.

Sign-off

  1. The octagon is universal in v1. Side count doesn't track word length — that's already in the foot, and 16 px won't hold more than three distinguishable silhouettes anyway. The open question is whether the silhouette should later encode grammatical class (thing / place / condition), which is the one variable worth it.
  2. The margin sets the chamfer, not the other way round. A 3 px margin and a deep octagon eat the same corners, and the figure needs all twelve usable rows. At chamfer 3 the top and bottom rows shrink to six columns — too few for the foot. Chamfer 2 leaves eight, which is the deepest cut that still clears. Going deeper means redrawing the letter-forms shorter.
  3. Pigment is redundant, and has to stay that way. Name-hashed hue, renormalised to a single luminance so every groove cuts the same depth; all 52 runes verified still distinct with colour stripped. If a future change ever makes two glyphs tell apart only by hue, that change is wrong.
  4. Homographs are a language bug, not an art bug. The mark is (letter 1, letter 3, length tally), so TERRA and TVRBA render identically. The datapack validator must reject that at load; the fix is a synonym — which is why a throng is GREX.
  5. Stone palette — one material now, in both page themes, because the texture that ships is a single image. Tune the two endpoints to match whatever the tablets end up looking like.
  6. Tier 1 (Sighted) = a shallow, unfilled scratch — settled, and it closes the last gap left when the frame went away. The three tiers are now three depths of cut, which is a value ladder before it is a colour one.
